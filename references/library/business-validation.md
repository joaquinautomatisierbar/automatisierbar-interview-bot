# Business Validation

> Frameworks for testing business ideas before scaling them — hypothesizing what could break the idea, designing the cheapest experiment that produces real evidence, and deciding whether to persevere, pivot, or kill.

**Sources distilled:** Testing Business Ideas (David J. Bland & Alex Osterwalder, Strategyzer / Wiley, 2020).
**Companion books referenced (not distilled here):** Business Model Generation (Osterwalder, 2010), Value Proposition Design (Osterwalder, 2014).

---

## Frameworks

### Idea → Search & Testing → Execution — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

**Three states of business risk:**

| State | What you're doing | Risk profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Idea** | "Wouldn't it be great if…" | Maximum uncertainty, zero evidence |
| **Search & Testing** | Run experiments to surface evidence | Uncertainty actively decreasing per experiment |
| **Execution** | Scale a validated business | Uncertainty low — risks now are operational |

The mistake every entrepreneur makes: **executing ideas prematurely** because the spreadsheet looks great, the deck is convincing, the business plan reads well — only to learn later that the vision was a hallucination.

> *"Testing is the activity of reducing the risk of pursuing ideas that look good in theory, but won't work in reality."*

**The entrepreneur's #1 task: reduce risk and uncertainty.** Speed of learning > speed of building.

---

### The Three Risks of Any Business Idea — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

Every business idea breaks down into hypotheses about three risks. You test each independently — and a winning idea must pass all three.

| Risk | Question | What it means if it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do customers actually want this? | Market too small, value prop weak, can't acquire/retain. **Most ideas die here.** |
| **Feasibility** | Can we actually build and deliver it? | Missing tech, IP, brand, or partners. Can't execute. |
| **Viability** | Can we earn enough money? | Revenue too low, costs too high, customers won't pay enough. |

**Test order matters:** start with **desirability** before investing in feasibility or viability. There's no point validating that you *can* build something nobody wants.

### Discovery vs Validation — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

**Two phases of search & testing**, distinguished by the type of evidence you're collecting and the strength of decisions you're qualified to make.

| Phase | Goal | Evidence quality | Decisions enabled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | "Are we directionally right?" | Weak: interviews, surveys, observation. Many false positives. | Course-correction, scope changes, pivots |
| **Validation** | "Confirm this is very likely to work" | Strong: real customer money, real signups, real behavior. Hard to fake. | Scaling, hiring, fundraising |

**Common mistake:** treating Discovery evidence ("12 people said they'd pay $X in interviews") as Validation evidence. **Talk is cheap. Cash is data.** Customers say nice things to be polite. Validation requires actions with real cost (signups, payments, time investment).

### The Business Model Canvas (9 blocks) — *Source: Osterwalder, referenced in Testing Business Ideas*

A one-page map of the entire business model. Each block is a **separate cluster of hypotheses** to test.

| Block | What it describes |
|---|---|
| **Customer Segments** | Different groups of people or organizations you aim to reach and serve. |
| **Value Propositions** | The bundle of products and services that creates value for a specific customer segment. |
| **Channels** | How you communicate with and reach customer segments to deliver a value proposition. |
| **Customer Relationships** | The types of relationships you establish with specific customer segments. |
| **Revenue Streams** | The cash you generate from each customer segment. |
| **Key Resources** | The most important assets required to make the business model work. |
| **Key Activities** | The most important things you must do to make the business model work. |
| **Key Partners** | The network of suppliers and partners that make the business model work. |
| **Cost Structure** | All costs incurred to operate the business model. |

**Operator use:** before you start testing, fill the canvas with assumptions. Then circle the riskiest 3-5 hypotheses (the ones that, if false, would kill the business). Test those first.

### The Value Proposition Canvas (Customer Fit) — *Source: Osterwalder, referenced in Testing Business Ideas*

A zoom-in on Customer Segments + Value Propositions. Two halves:

**Customer Profile** (what's true about the customer regardless of you):
- **Customer Jobs** — what they're trying to get done in their work and life
- **Pains** — bad outcomes, risks, obstacles related to those jobs
- **Gains** — benefits they want, outcomes they seek

**Value Map** (what you offer to fit the profile):
- **Products & Services** — what you sell
- **Pain Relievers** — how your offering alleviates customer pains
- **Gain Creators** — how your offering creates customer gains

**Fit happens** when your Pain Relievers + Gain Creators match the customer's actual Pains + Gains. Most pre-product businesses build a Value Map without first profiling the Customer side — and end up with elegant solutions to nobody's problems.

### The 4-Step Test Loop — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

| Step | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hypothesize** | State the riskiest assumption as a falsifiable hypothesis. "If [we do X], then [Y will happen], because [Z]." | A testable claim with a measurable success threshold |
| **2. Experiment** | Pick the cheapest experiment that produces evidence about the hypothesis. (See "Experiment Types" below.) | Real data |
| **3. Learn** | Compare the result to the success threshold. What's the new insight? | Calibrated belief about the hypothesis |
| **4. Decide** | Persevere (continue testing the next risk), pivot (change something fundamental), or kill (drop the idea). | Direction for the next loop |

**The loop runs repeatedly.** Discovery loops generate weak signal; validation loops generate strong signal. Move from discovery → validation as evidence accumulates.

### Experiment Type Categories — *Source: Testing Business Ideas (44 experiments distilled)*

The book catalogs 44 experiments grouped by phase. Pick the **cheapest experiment that meaningfully reduces the risk** at hand. Type-by-type sketch:

**Discovery experiments (cheap, fast, weak signal):**
- **Customer interviews** — open-ended Jobs/Pains/Gains discovery. Avoid leading questions; use "tell me about a time when…"
- **Online surveys** — quantify opinion at scale. Beware: opinions ≠ behavior.
- **Search trends analysis** — Google Trends, autocomplete. Reveals existing demand magnitude.
- **Discussion forum search** — Reddit, niche subforums. Reveals language customers actually use.
- **Web traffic analytics** on a landing page — clicks, time-on-page, scroll-depth.
- **Card sorting** — for IA / feature-priority discovery.
- **Speed-boating** — facilitated workshop where participants prioritize jobs/pains/gains.

**Validation experiments (more cost, stronger signal):**
- **Pre-sales** — "Pay $X now to reserve, ship in N weeks." Real money is the strongest desirability + viability signal.
- **Crowdfunding campaigns** — Kickstarter / Indiegogo as a validated demand mechanism.
- **Letter of intent (B2B)** — non-binding written commitment. Real signature ≠ verbal yes.
- **Concierge MVP** — manually deliver the value to one customer using zero automation. Validates desirability + reveals the operational shape of the future product.
- **Wizard of Oz** — front-end looks automated; back-end is humans. Validates the value without building the system.
- **Single-feature MVP** — strip the product to its one most-used feature. Tests if that one feature alone delivers enough value.
- **Mash-up MVP** — duct-tape existing products together to deliver the experience. Validates desirability before building bespoke tech.
- **Imposter judo / "fake-door" test** — landing page with a feature that doesn't exist; click-through to "coming soon." Measures real demand.
- **Explainer video** — pre-product video describing the offering. Dropbox's famous validation.

**Strong-signal pricing experiments:**
- **A/B price test** — show different prices to different cohorts; measure conversion + LTV.
- **Storyboards / discovery boards** — visualize the user journey at price X to find drop-off points.

**Feasibility experiments:**
- **Spike / technical prototype** — narrow technical proof of one risky component. Time-boxed.
- **Partner LOI** — non-binding partnership letter to validate channel feasibility before committing engineering.
- **Day in the life** — shadow the customer to validate they have the workflow gap your product fills.

**Viability experiments:**
- **Cost simulation** — model unit economics with real input data.
- **Pricing-power test** — raise the price by 25%; measure conversion impact.

**Critical principle:** match experiment cost to the risk being tested. Don't run a $50K validation experiment when a $500 interview round would tell you whether the assumption is even worth validating.

### Strength of Evidence Hierarchy — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

When you collect data, weigh it by quality. Strongest at the top:

| Strength | Evidence type |
|---|---|
| **Strongest** | Customer paid real money for the thing |
| | Customer signed a binding contract |
| | Customer built workarounds (DIY-ed your solution before you existed) |
| | Customer changed observable behavior (usage data, repeat purchase) |
| | Customer verbally committed to a specific dollar amount + date |
| | Customer expressed problem-pain in their own words, unprompted |
| | Customer answered survey saying they'd buy |
| | Customer said "this is great" in an interview |
| **Weakest** | Customer nodded politely while you pitched |

**Translation:** any evidence at the bottom should be discounted heavily. Make decisions on evidence at the top.

### Team Behaviors That Predict Validation Success — *Source: Testing Business Ideas*

Six behaviors of teams that consistently validate ideas faster:

| # | Behavior | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Data-influenced** (not data-driven) | Insights from data shape strategy, but data alone doesn't decide. Pair quantitative + qualitative. |
| 2 | **Experiment-driven** | Willing to be wrong. Match experiment to riskiest assumption; don't just keep building features. |
| 3 | **Customer-centric** | Always connected to the customer, not just at "the new customer experience." |
| 4 | **Entrepreneurial** | Sense of urgency. Move fast and validate things. |
| 5 | **Iterative approach** | Repeat the loop. Don't fall in love with the first idea. |
| 6 | **Question assumptions** | Willing to test disruptive models, not just safe ones. |

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- **Talk is cheap; cash is data.** A signature is stronger than a verbal yes; a payment is stronger than a signature; repeat usage is stronger than a payment.
- **Match experiment cost to risk magnitude.** Don't spend $50K validating something a $500 experiment could falsify.
- **Test desirability before feasibility before viability.** Reverse order is the most common (and most expensive) mistake.
- **Discovery insights ≠ validation evidence.** Don't make scaling decisions on Discovery data.
- **The cheapest validation is a pre-sale.** Real money + real commitment + real customer = the strongest possible signal short of full delivery.
- **Concierge before code.** Manually deliver the value to 1-3 customers before automating anything. You'll learn 10× more about the actual problem shape.
- **Wizard-of-Oz before bespoke build.** If humans can deliver the back-end manually for a few customers, you don't need automation yet.
- **Single-feature MVP > full-featured MVP.** Strip to the one most-used feature. Validate or kill that. Then add.
- **Six years and 51 failed games preceded Angry Birds.** Most successful businesses are the founder's nth attempt, not their first. Don't let one failed test discourage you; let it inform the next one.
- **Three commonly-required testing skills:** design, product, engineering. If your team is missing one, partner external before solo-validating.
- **Diverse team = better testing.** Homogeneous teams bake their biases into the business. Hard to rectify later.
- **Avoid premature scaling.** A business that hasn't validated desirability + viability + feasibility cannot benefit from scale. It just amplifies the underlying problem.
- **Customer access is non-negotiable.** A team that can't talk to real customers will guess and build. Guess-and-build is the fastest path to a beautiful failure.
- **Lead with questions, not answers** (especially when the leader doesn't know the solution). The bottleneck is always at the top of the bottle.
- **The smallest dedicated team beats a large multitasking team.** Multitasking across projects silently kills validation progress.
- **Time-box every experiment.** Open-ended experiments become projects; projects become products that nobody asked for.

## Anti-patterns (avoid)

- **Falling in love with the first idea.** Generate many before you commit.
- **Building before validating.** "We can't validate without building" is almost always wrong — Wizard-of-Oz and concierge MVPs validate without building.
- **Survey-only validation.** Surveys measure stated preferences, which often diverge wildly from revealed behavior.
- **Running interviews with leading questions.** "Wouldn't you love to have X?" produces useless data. "Tell me about a time when…" produces useful data.
- **Treating polite "yes" responses as commitments.** Customers want to be nice. Test for behavior, not pleasantries.
- **Conflating advisor / friend / family feedback with customer feedback.** They have a relationship bias. Discount accordingly.
- **Investing in feasibility before desirability.** Building tech for a product nobody wants is the most expensive failure mode.
- **Treating the business plan as the truth.** It's a hypothesis document. Run it through the validation loop.
- **Rejecting experimentation because "we already know."** That confidence is exactly what testing reduces. The teams that "already know" are the ones who waste the most.
- **Demanding success from every experiment.** Failure is the goal of weak hypotheses — it's how you learn cheaply. Punishing failure makes the team risk-averse, which guarantees larger failures later.
- **Multitasking across multiple validation projects.** Cuts the experiment cycle time and degrades quality of evidence on each.
- **Starving the validation team.** Teams need budget, customer access, and decision-making autonomy. Without these, they generate compliance, not evidence.
- **Doing all experiments in-house.** Pre-built tools (landing pages, online ads, no-code MVP platforms) let you run validation cheaper and faster.

## Worked examples / case studies

- **Rovio's Angry Birds** — preceded by 6 years and **51 failed games**. Each failure was a small, cheap, validated learning. Eventually one survived. The portfolio approach: many tiny bets, each tested cheaply, one wins big.
- **Dropbox's explainer video MVP** — pre-built, the team made a 3-minute video showing how Dropbox would work. Posted to Hacker News. Beta signups jumped from 5K → 75K overnight. The validation: massive desirability for the value prop, *before* shipping a single line of code at scale.
- **Zappos's concierge MVP** — Nick Swinmurn went to local shoe stores, photographed shoes, posted them online. When orders came in, he went *back* to the stores, bought the shoes, shipped them. Validated demand for online shoe-buying without building any inventory or fulfillment system. Eventually sold to Amazon for $1.2B.
- **Buffer's two-page landing test** — prelaunch, the founder put up a "pricing" page with monthly tiers and a checkout button. Clicking the button revealed "we're not live yet — leave your email." Measured conversion intent at each price point. Validated viability *before* building the product.
